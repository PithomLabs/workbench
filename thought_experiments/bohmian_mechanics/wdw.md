The conundrum exists because generations of physicists absorbed a philosophical premise so deeply that it became invisible: *that quantum mechanics is fundamentally about observation.* That reality is indefinite until observed. That the theory describes what we can *know* rather than what *is.*

Bohmian mechanics resolves the conundrum by rejecting this premise entirely. It says: quantum mechanics is a theory of matter in motion. The wave function is the law of motion. The configuration is the matter. Reality is always definite. Observers are just complicated bits of the configuration. Measurement is just correlation. Time is just the configuration's trajectory read through internal structure.

The resolution doesn't require new physics, new mathematics, or new formalism. It requires a single philosophical move: *taking the ontology seriously.* Treating the wave function and the configuration as *real things* rather than calculational tools. Once you make that move, the measurement problem, the observer problem, the cosmological problem, the problem of time — they all dissolve. Not because they're answered with new mechanisms, but because they were artifacts of a framework that mistook our limitations for features of reality.

The cost isn't what people usually think it is. The cost isn't nonlocality or determinism or high-dimensional configuration space. The cost is giving up the idea that observation is fundamental to physics. That's not a scientific cost — it's a philosophical one. And the reward for paying it is a quantum mechanics that works everywhere, for everything, including the universe itself, without ever needing to invoke an observer who was never there.

## 1. The conundrum, reframed

- **Copenhagen:** Add an external observer who collapses the wave function.  
- **Many‑Worlds:** No collapse; all outcomes are real in different branches.  
- **Objective collapse:** Make the wave function itself collapse randomly at some scale.

All three share the same starting point: the wave function is the *whole* story, and you have to add something extra (observers, branches, or collapse hits) to get definite outcomes.

Bohmian mechanics starts from a different premise: the wave function is *not* the whole story. There is always a definite configuration of “beables” (e.g. particle positions, or 3‑geometry + matter fields) in addition to the wave function. The wave function guides the evolution of that configuration, but the configuration is always there, and the universal wave function never collapses.【turn4search0】

So the conundrum “who collapsed the first wave function?” simply doesn’t arise. The universe was never in an indefinite superposition waiting for a collapse; it was always in a definite configuration, guided by a wave function.

## 2. What Bohmian mechanics positively says about the universe

### 2.1 No collapse, ever

## 3. The imaginative move: how Bohmian mechanics “neatly resolves” the conundrum

Let’s put on the thought‑experiment hat and see where Bohmian mechanics really shines compared to the other interpretations.

### 3.1 Copenhagen: the universe is a measurement without an observer

Copenhagen says: quantum systems don’t have definite properties until they are “measured,” and measurement requires an external classical apparatus/observer. Apply that to the universe as a whole and you get:

- No external observer → no measurement → no definite reality.  
- The universe can’t “happen” until someone looks.

You can try to patch this with:

- **Consciousness‑causes‑collapse:** the first conscious mind collapses the universe.  
- **Participatory universe (Wheeler):** observers now retroactively collapse the early universe.

Bohmian mechanics simply rejects the premise: there is no special role for “measurement” or observers at all. The universe is always in a definite configuration; the wave function guides its evolution. Observers are just patterns inside that configuration, not external agencies that bring reality into being.

### 3.2 Many‑Worlds: the universe is a branching, observer‑less block

Many‑Worlds says: the universal wave function never collapses, and all possible outcomes are realized in different branches. The “world” we experience is just one branch.

The cost:

- You must accept that all outcomes are equally real, even though we only ever see one.  
- You must explain why “I” am in *this* branch and not another, which quickly runs into difficult issues of probability and self‑location.

Bohmian mechanics keeps the universal wave function and its branching structure, but it adds a *single* actual configuration that “selects” one branch as the real one, the one occupied by the actual particles/fields.【turn4search0】【turn4search2】 The other branches are empty of configuration. So from the point of view of the thought‑experiment:

- Many‑Worlds says: “All branches are real; you’re just in one of them.”  
- Bohm says: “All branches are in the wave function, but only one is occupied by the actual configuration. That’s the one you’re in.”

The conundrum “who collapsed the first wave function?” is replaced by: “why is *this* branch the occupied one?” Bohmian mechanics can answer: that’s just how the initial configuration was set. No collapse, no special observer, no branching of reality—just a single, definite history.

### 3.3 Objective collapse: the universe is a random hit

Objective collapse models (GRW, Penrose, etc.) modify the Schrödinger equation to add spontaneous collapses. The early universe could have collapsed itself without observers.

The cost:

- You must modify the dynamics (and thus the experimental predictions) at some scale.  
- You still have to explain why the collapse hits *this* particular outcome and not another.  
- You still have no deeper explanation for why there is a definite outcome at all; it’s just put in by hand.

Bohmian mechanics keeps the standard linear dynamics for the universal wave function and moves the “definiteness” into the configuration, which is always there. No extra collapse mechanism is needed; the appearance of collapse is just decoherence + ignoring empty branches.
