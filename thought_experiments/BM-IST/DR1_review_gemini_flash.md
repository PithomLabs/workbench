### 1. Attack on Route B: The Quantum Stress vs. Classical Reynolds Stress

In classical kinetic theory, the continuum limit of an ensemble of trajectories with distribution function $f(x, v, t) \ge 0$ is governed by the moment hierarchy of the collisionless Boltzmann (Vlasov) equation:
$$\partial_t \rho + \nabla \cdot (\rho \bar{v}) = 0,$$
$$\partial_t (\rho \bar{v}_i) + \sum_j \partial_j (\rho \bar{v}_i \bar{v}_j + \mathbb{P}_{ij}) = -\rho \partial_i V_{\rm ext},$$
where $\bar{v}(x,t) = \frac{1}{\rho}\int v f(x,v,t)\,dv$, and the **Reynolds stress / kinetic dispersion tensor** is defined as:
$$\mathbb{P}_{ij}(x, t) \equiv \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} (v_i - \bar{v}_i)(v_j - \bar{v}_j) f(x, v, t) \, dv.$$

#### The Target Quantum Hydrodynamic Identity
In Madelung quantum hydrodynamics (and the Euler–Korteweg system), the momentum equation is:
$$\partial_t (\rho v_i) + \sum_j \partial_j (\rho v_i v_j) = -\rho \partial_i V_{\rm ext} - \rho \partial_i Q,$$
where $Q = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac{\nabla^2 \sqrt{\rho}}{\sqrt{\rho}}$. The quantum force density can be written as the divergence of the **quantum stress tensor** $T^Q$:
$$\rho \partial_i Q = \sum_j \partial_j T^Q_{ij}, \qquad T^Q_{ij} = \frac{\hbar^2}{4m} \rho \, \partial_i \partial_j \ln \rho = \frac{\hbar^2}{4m} \left( \partial_i \partial_j \rho - \frac{1}{\rho}\partial_i \rho \partial_j \rho \right).$$
*(Note: An alternative form differs by an isotropic term $\frac{\hbar^2}{4m}\nabla^2\rho \, \delta_{ij}$, but the divergence $\partial_j T^Q_{ij}$ is identical).*

To establish Route B, the coarse-grained microscopic stress $\mathbb{P}_{ij}$ must converge to the quantum stress:
$$\mathbb{P}_{ij}(x, t) \stackrel{?}{=} - T^Q_{ij}(x, t) = -\frac{\hbar^2}{4m} \rho(x, t) \, \partial_i \partial_j \ln \rho(x, t).$$

### 2. A Crucial Structural Obstacle: Hudson’s Positivity Theorem

Analyzing this equality reveals a fundamental algebraic and measure-theoretic constraint:

#### 1. Positive Semi-Definiteness of Classical Stress
Because the microscopic measure $\mu$ on $I_U$ is a genuine probability measure, the coarse-grained phase-space distribution $f(x, v, t)$ is non-negative everywhere ($f \ge 0$). Consequently, for any vector $\xi \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the contraction with $\mathbb{P}$ is a variance:
$$\xi^T \mathbb{P}(x, t) \xi = \int (\xi \cdot (v - \bar{v}))^2 f(x, v, t) \, dv \ge 0.$$
**The classical kinetic stress tensor $\mathbb{P}(x, t)$ is positive semi-definite at every point $x$.**

#### 2. Indefinite Sign of the Quantum Stress Tensor
Now examine the candidate tensor:
$$\mathbb{P}_{ij} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{4m} \rho \, \partial_i \partial_j \ln \rho.$$
For $\mathbb{P}$ to be positive semi-definite, the matrix $\partial_i \partial_j \ln \rho(x)$ must be **negative semi-definite everywhere**, meaning that $\ln \rho(x)$ must be a globally **concave** function on $\mathbb{R}^d$.
- **The Gaussian case:** If $\rho(x) \propto e^{-|x|^2 / 2\sigma^2}$, then $\ln \rho(x) = C - \frac{|x|^2}{2\sigma^2}$, so $\partial_i \partial_j \ln \rho = -\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\delta_{ij} \le 0$. Here, $-T^Q_{ij} \ge 0$, and the tensor is positive definite.
- **General quantum states:** For virtually any non-Gaussian quantum state—such as interference fringes $\rho(x) \propto \cos^2(kx)$, heavy-tailed distributions $\rho(x) \propto (1 + x^4)^{-1}$ (where $\partial_x^2 \ln \rho > 0$ for $|x| > 3^{-1/4}$), or wave packets with inflection points and nodal lines—$\partial_i \partial_j \ln \rho$ has positive eigenvalues.
- Where $\partial_i \partial_j \ln \rho > 0$, the required classical variance would have to be **strictly negative**:
  $$\xi^T \mathbb{P} \xi < 0 \quad \text{(impossible for any real ensemble of trajectories)}.$$

#### 3. Connection to Hudson’s Theorem (1974)
This is the hydrodynamic manifestation of **Hudson’s Theorem** (Hudson, 1974, *Rep. Math. Phys.*):
> *The only pure quantum states whose Wigner quasi-probability distribution $W(x, p)$ is non-negative everywhere are Gaussian states.*

Because the Wigner distribution $W(x, p)$ is the unique phase-space distribution whose second velocity moments yield the quantum stress tensor $T^Q$, and because $W(x, p)$ is necessarily negative for any state exhibiting quantum interference, **no non-negative phase-space distribution $f(x, v) \ge 0$ generated by deterministic trajectories can reproduce the local quantum stress tensor $T^Q_{ij}(x)$ for general states.**

### 3. How Can Route B Survive Hudson's Theorem?

If a direct point-by-point identification $\mathbb{P}_{ij}(x) = -T^Q_{ij}(x)$ is impossible for non-Gaussian states, how can Route B be formulated without violating measure positivity?

In the literature of Hall & Reginatto (2002) and Caticha (2019), the quantum potential does not arise as a pointwise kinetic dispersion tensor of a single fluid. Instead, it emerges in one of two ways:

#### Mechanism B.1: The Two-Fluid / Osmotic Decomposition
Following Nelson (1985) and the two-fluid Madelung formulation:
The total microscopic velocity of a trajectory is decomposed into a **current velocity** $v(x, t)$ and an **osmotic velocity** $u(x, t)$:
$$v_{\rm micro} = v \pm u, \qquad u(x, t) \equiv \frac{\hbar}{2m} \nabla \ln \rho(x, t).$$
The momentum density has two components. When calculating the net acceleration, the stress tensor is not simply $\rho \langle \delta v \delta v \rangle$; rather, the osmotic momentum flux enters the Euler equation with an opposite sign through the stochastic/forward-backward acceleration:
$$a = \frac{d v}{dt} - \frac{d u}{dt_{\rm backward}} \Longrightarrow \partial_t v + (v \cdot \nabla) v = -(u \cdot \nabla) u - \frac{\hbar}{2m}\nabla^2 u - \frac{1}{m}\nabla V.$$
Using $u = \frac{\hbar}{2m} \frac{\nabla \rho}{\rho}$, a vector identity gives:
$$-(u \cdot \nabla) u - \frac{\hbar}{2m} \nabla^2 u = -\frac{1}{m} \nabla Q.$$
*Consequence for BM-IST:* The invariant set cannot be treated as a single classical fluid whose raw velocity dispersion equals $T^Q$. It would have to possess a **duality or time-reversal pairing of trajectories** (a forward and backward bundle on $I_U$) whose difference defines the osmotic field $u$.

#### Mechanism B.2: Non-Local Action of Fisher Information
Hall & Reginatto (2002) showed that the quantum potential is derived from the **Fisher information functional**:
$$I_F[\rho] = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \rho(x) |\nabla \ln \rho(x)|^2 \, dx = 4 \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} |\nabla \sqrt{\rho(x)}|^2 \, dx.$$
The exact variation of the Fisher information with respect to $\rho$ gives:
$$\frac{\delta}{\delta \rho(x)} \left( \frac{\hbar^2}{8m} I_F[\rho] \right) = Q(x) = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac{\nabla^2 \sqrt{\rho}}{\sqrt{\rho}}.$$
Under this mechanism, $Q(x)$ is not a local moment of velocity dispersion at point $x$. It is the **functional derivative of an ensemble information metric**.

*What BM-IST Must Prove for Mechanism B.2:*
It is not enough to show that trajectories disperse. One must show that the measure $\mu$ on $I_U$ minimizes or constrains the coordinate uncertainty via an **Exact Uncertainty Principle**:
$$\mathcal{E}_{\rm ensemble} = \int \left( \frac{1}{2}\rho m v^2 + \rho V \right) dx + \frac{\hbar^2}{8m} I_F[\rho].$$
To derive this from IST, the geometry of the Cantor fibers $\mathcal{C}$ must force the microstates to have an informational density fluctuation cost proportional to the Fisher information.
