The Universe Is Not a Hologram
Leonard Susskind — one of the principal developers of the Holographic Principle — has himself said:
“The trouble is, the universe that we live in is not the anti-de-Sitter geometry. It’s the de-Sitter geometry. And we don’t really understand its quantum mechanics at all.”

Digging deeper: Why AdS/CFT Does Not Establish Holographic Reality
The Holographic Principle is one of the most seductive ideas in modern theoretical physics. At its strongest, it suggests that everything occurring within a volume of space can ultimately be represented by information residing on a lower-dimensional boundary — rather like a three-dimensional image encoded on a two-dimensional hologram.
There are reasons why physicists take holography seriously. But there is a much weaker case for the popular conclusion that our universe actually is a hologram. The distinction matters.
1. AdS/CFT is a duality, not proof of what reality is made of
The strongest concrete realization of holography is Maldacena’s AdS/CFT correspondence:
Gravity in a (d + 1)-dimensional AdS spacetime ↔ a d-dimensional conformal field theory
This is an extraordinary result. But suppose two theories provide mathematically equivalent descriptions of the same physics. It does not follow that one description is physically real while the other is merely an illusion.
If A ≡ B, we cannot simply conclude that B is fundamental and A emerges from it.
AdS/CFT may establish an extraordinary equivalence between descriptions without telling us which — if either — represents the fundamental ontology. Indeed, exact duality arguably weakens rather than strengthens the claim that one dimensional description is privileged.
2. There is an inconvenient problem: our universe is not AdS
The best-understood holographic correspondence applies to anti-de Sitter spacetime, which has negative cosmological constant and a particularly useful asymptotic boundary. Our observed accelerating universe is instead approximately described by a positive cosmological constant, making its large-scale geometry much closer to de Sitter spacetime.
That difference is not cosmetic. The structure that makes the AdS holographic dictionary work so elegantly is absent or radically different in de Sitter spacetime. Research programmes attempting to construct de Sitter and flat-space holography certainly exist, but these remain extensions beyond the mature AdS/CFT framework rather than equivalently established theories.
So:
Holography in AdS ≠ Holography of our Universe.
That extrapolation remains conjectural.
3. Black-hole area laws do not prove that reality lives on surfaces
Much of the original motivation for holography comes from black-hole thermodynamics. The Bekenstein–Hawking relation gives:
Sᴮᴴ = kᴮc³A / 4Gℏ
The remarkable feature is that black-hole entropy scales with the area of the horizon rather than its volume. That is profoundly important.
But S ∝ A does not logically imply that fundamental degrees of freedom physically reside on the boundary.
Area scaling could instead arise because gravity severely constrains how many independent physical states can exist within a region, because horizons limit accessible information, because of quantum entanglement, or because gravitational systems contain redundancies absent from ordinary local field theories.
Black-hole thermodynamics establishes the area law remarkably well theoretically, but the ontological interpretation of what those degrees of freedom represent remains a deeper question.
4. Entropy bounds are not themselves the Holographic Principle
Black-hole physics motivated limits on how much entropy or information could occupy a spacetime region. Those ideas eventually helped inspire ’t Hooft and Susskind’s Holographic Principle.
But the historical route from entropy bounds to universal holography is not compulsory.
The logical sequence is better expressed as:
Black-hole thermodynamics ↓ area-dependent entropy and entropy bounds ↓ motivation for holography
—not—
proof that the universe is two-dimensional underneath.
5. Information is not the same thing as physical existence
Suppose every physically observable property of a volume could be completely encoded on its boundary. That would be remarkable. But encoding something does not mean that the encoded thing physically exists inside its encoding medium.
A library catalogue may contain enough information to locate every book. The catalogue is not the library.
A mathematical state vector may encode everything that can be predicted about a system. The system is not therefore literally made from the symbols in the state vector.
Likewise:
boundary representation of bulk physics ≠ bulk physics physically located on the boundary.
The Holographic Principle becomes metaphysically much stronger whenever representability is silently converted into constitution.
6. Holography does not eliminate the bulk — it has to reconstruct it
If the boundary description really is fundamental, holographic theories must explain why apparently local bulk spacetime emerges so convincingly.
Modern AdS/CFT uses sophisticated ideas involving entanglement wedges, quantum error correction and nonlocal reconstruction of bulk observables from boundary degrees of freedom. That is an impressive achievement.
But notice what has happened. We began with a theory in which locality is obvious: objects occupy locations in spacetime. We replace it with a lower-dimensional description in which locality is obscured, then construct elaborate mechanisms to recover the locality with which we started.
That may reveal extraordinarily deep mathematics. It does not, by itself, prove that the nonlocal description is more physically real.
7. The success of duality may support structural realism instead
Perhaps neither side of the correspondence deserves privileged ontological status. If radically different mathematical descriptions encode precisely the same physics, what is fundamental may instead be the relational structure common to both.
Bulk ↔ Boundary does not mean Bulk is secretly Boundary.
It may simply mean that nature admits different representations of the same underlying physical structure.
This is a more modest — and arguably more philosophically defensible — conclusion.
8. There is still no direct observation that our universe possesses a holographic boundary
Perhaps most importantly, nobody has experimentally detected the putative lower-dimensional substrate of our universe.
We observe three macroscopic spatial dimensions, local quantum fields, and gravitational dynamics remarkably well described by four-dimensional general relativity. None of these observations requires us to interpret our experienced spacetime as a projection from a lower-dimensional boundary.
Holography may eventually provide testable predictions that distinguish it from alternative theories of quantum gravity. At present, however, its strongest support is theoretical rather than the direct observation of a holographic substrate.
Theoretical elegance is not the same thing as empirical confirmation.
The conceptual mistake
The deepest mistake may therefore be surprisingly simple: holography risks confusing a limit on information with a claim about existence.
Gravity may constrain how many independently distinguishable states can inhabit a region. A lower-dimensional theory may even provide a complete mathematical description of those states. Neither fact requires the conclusion that the higher-dimensional world is unreal.
Black-hole area law ⇒ deep constraint on information
AdS/CFT ⇒ remarkable bulk–boundary duality
but neither currently gives us:
Our universe literally exists on a lower-dimensional boundary.
