What a rich commission. The gap between Acts 1 and Revelation 22 spans roughly six decades — and most of it is told only in fragments: a slave girl set free in one verse, a young man falling from a window, a companion "left sick" in a single line. Those silences are exactly where historical fiction does its best work. Here are the twelve posts I'd write, in roughly chronological order, each built on an overlooked person or moment.

---

## 1. The Girl Who Followed — Philippi, c. AD 50
**Anchor:** Acts 16:16–18
She is the first person Paul meets in Europe — a demon-possessed slave girl who follows the apostles for days, shouting the truth. Paul frees her, and she vanishes from the record while the riot over her lost income makes history. I'd tell her story: what she did with freedom no one tracked, and what it costs when your deliverance makes other people poorer.

## 2. The House That Held a Church — Corinth and Rome, c. 50–60
**Anchor:** Acts 18:1–3, 18–26; Romans 16:3–5
Priscilla and Aquila, tentmakers who hosted churches in at least three cities and "risked their necks" for Paul (Rom 16:4). The untold thread: Priscilla teaching the eloquent Apollos more accurately — a woman instructing a preacher in a patriarchal world, and the church growing through kitchens and workshops, not pulpits.

## 3. The Boy Who Fell Asleep — Troas, c. AD 57
**Anchor:** Acts 20:7–12
Eutychus dozes during a midnight sermon, falls three stories, and is "taken up dead" — then restored. On the surface it's an odd miracle story. Told from the family's side, it's about the terror of losing a child in a crowded upper room, and the relief of discovering the church is a family that weeps together, breaks bread together, and grieves at dawn.

## 4. Trophimus, Left Behind — Miletus, c. AD 66–67
**Anchor:** 2 Timothy 4:20
Paul's faithful traveling companion, mentioned in Acts and 2 Corinthians, simply left sick at Miletus while Paul races toward execution. One sentence in Scripture — but what did it feel like to be abandoned by your spiritual father for the sake of the mission? A meditation on the ones left behind, and on God keeping those we cannot.

## 5. Demas: The Anatomy of a Defection — Rome, c. 60–67
**Anchor:** Colossians 4:14; Philemon 24; 2 Timothy 4:10
Trace him across three letters: trusted coworker, then "Demas has deserted me, because he loved this present world." No scandal, no persecution — just a slow cooling. The tragedy post: how love of the age quietly displaces love of the mission, and why Paul still names him without bitterness.

## 6. Onesimus: The Runaway Who Became a Son — Rome, c. AD 60
**Anchor:** Philemon; Colossians 4:9
A slave flees his master, meets Paul in chains, and becomes "useful" — his very name. The letter to Philemon is Paul's gamble: grace sent back into the household that owned him. The striking question: did Onesimus walk through that door freely, and what did reconciliation cost both men? (Early tradition says he became a bishop — a stunning vindication.)

## 7. Mark's Second Chance — Cyprus and Rome, c. 46–67
**Anchor:** Acts 13:13; 15:36–39; 2 Timothy 4:11
He deserted the mission young; Paul refused to take him back; Barnabas split the team over him. Decades later, Paul, awaiting death, asks: "Get Mark and bring him, for he is useful to me." The deserter had meanwhile written the first Gospel. The redemption arc every wounded ministry needs to hear.

## 8. The Year the City Burned — Rome, AD 64
**Anchor:** 1 Peter 4:12–19 (with Tacitus, *Annals* 15)
Nero's fire, and Christians wrapped in pitch, crucified, burned as lamps. Told through a household church meeting in the ashes — some fleeing, some staying, some renouncing. The post about public scapegoating: how a community answers slander not with violence but with suffering, and what it means to "glorify God in this name" while it costs everything.

## 9. The Long Siege — Jerusalem, AD 66–70
**Anchor:** Luke 21:20–24 as lived memory; Hebrews 10:32–39
The temple falls. Through the eyes of a believing Jewish family — perhaps one that remembered the words of Jesus on the Mount of Olives — the horror of famine inside the walls and the grief of watching the nation's heart torn down. The tragedy post: judgment and mercy interwoven, and why the early church read Hebrews beside the rubble.

## 10. The Prayers Under the Altar — The martyrs, c. AD 90s
**Anchor:** Revelation 6:9–11
"How long, O Lord?" The dead under the altar are given white robes and told to rest *a little longer*, until the number of their fellow servants is complete. Perhaps the most haunting verse in the New Testament: martyrs' prayers seemingly unanswered, honored instead with rest and a robe. The post about learning to wait inside God's patience.

## 11. Antipas, Whom Jesus Remembers — Pergamos, c. AD 90s
**Anchor:** Revelation 2:12–13
The only martyr Christ names in the seven letters — "my faithful witness, who was killed among you, where Satan's throne is." History forgot him entirely; Jesus kept his name. Set against Pergamos's towering altar to Zeus, the post asks what it means that when no monument, chronicle, or church record preserves you, the Son of God does.

## 12. The Exile Who Saw the Son of Man — Patmos, c. AD 95
**Anchor:** Revelation 1:9–20; 22:17–21
John, the last living eyewitness of Jesus, broken in a labor camp on a barren rock island under Domitian — and on "the Lord's day" he hears a trumpet voice and turns to see the glorified Christ. The finale: an old man's loneliness shattered by the revelation that the crucified one now holds the keys of death. Ending the series where hope began: *"Surely I am coming soon."*

---

**Series throughline:** each post closes with a short "Epistle to the Reader" — tying that era's suffering to the same four words you named: inspiration (ordinary faithfulness), faith (trust in what isn't seen), hope (resurrection and return), love (the willingness to stay, or to let go).

If you'd like, I can fully draft any one of these — Antipas (#11) or Trophimus (#4) would be the strongest openers, since their emotional hooks are the sharpest and least familiar. Which one speaks to you first?
